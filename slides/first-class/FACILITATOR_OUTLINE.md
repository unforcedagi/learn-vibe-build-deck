## What to compress from the voice note

The original walkthrough has three strong openings: course origin, personal biography, and course philosophy. Keep all three, but give each a distinct job:

- Origin establishes the question: programming is changing.
- Biography establishes why Aaron can hold the inquiry: he has moved through software, away from it, back through human systems, and into agentic building.
- Philosophy establishes the durable curriculum: cognition, communication, creativity.

The biography slide is a six-stop map. Tell one vivid moment and move on; do not narrate every stop.

## The first shared practice

This is the recommended “small thing” students do before leaving. It needs no account setup.

1. Three minutes alone: “Name one thing you want to make, change, or understand, and why it matters to you.”
2. Four minutes in pairs: the partner may only ask clarifying questions. No advice yet.
3. Invite three one-sentence signals from the room.
4. Put those signals into Codex and ask:

   > Cluster these intentions into three emerging learning edges for this class. Preserve the specificity of each person. Name what you are uncertain about. Do not invent consensus.

5. Ask the room: What did it flatten? What did it notice? What needs revision?
6. Revise once. Save the result as the class’s first shared artifact.

The point is not that AI writes the class plan. The point is to make the course’s operating loop visible: human signal, communication, AI proposal, human judgment.

## The stable weekly scaffold

After week one, every normal Studio Cycle has three visible parts:

1. **Artifact (4/10):** a sincere attempt another person can encounter.
2. **Human trace (3/10):** at least one paragraph in the student's own words about process, choices, friction, and learning.
3. **Studio share (3/10):** demos alternate — half the room each week (groups set in week 2). On demo weeks share anything alive in your practice; on off weeks, questions and useful feedback.

Final course weights (matching Canvas and the syllabus): 50% Weekly Homework, 20% Attendance & Sharing, 30% Final Project + Showcase. This gives undergraduates a legible path through an intentionally adaptive course. Scores track visible completion and participation; narrative feedback points toward the next attempt.

## Week-one assignment: intention baseline

Three paragraphs in the student’s own words:

- My relationship with AI right now—how I use it, how it has grown, how I have felt impacted—and what I already know how to make.
- What do I want to become capable of, and why does it matter to me?
- What might I make, and what obstacles or support can I already see?

AI may ask questions, but the submitted prose should be the student's. No separate artifact is required; the intention statement itself is the first low-stakes artifact. It becomes the baseline Aaron can revisit at midterm and the end of the semester.

## Attendance recommendation

Give two no-fault studio passes with no documentation or explanation required. After those passes, avoid an automatic letter-grade cliff. Use the 20-point Studio Presence category instead:

- an uncommunicated missed studio after the two passes removes 5 points from that category;
- a student who communicates before class or as soon as reasonably possible may recover credit through a make-up demo, recorded or office-hours share, or useful feedback on classmates' work;
- illness, formal accommodations, and religious observances follow CU policy and should not be penalized.

This still makes sharing and presence consequential while giving students a visible, humane recovery path.

## Language changes I recommend

- Instead of “fewer engineers are even reading code,” say: “In my own circle, I see more experienced engineers spending their time orchestrating systems and agents rather than typing every line. Their earlier architectural experience still matters. The question is how we begin developing that judgment now.”
- Instead of promising a passing grade for an “I cannot do this” submission, say: “If life interrupts the work, communicate honestly. We will make a recovery plan. Silence is harder to work with than an unfinished attempt.”
- Instead of “easy grader,” say: “I care about engagement, attempts, communication, and growth—not polish for its own sake.”
